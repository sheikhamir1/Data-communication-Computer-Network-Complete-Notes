Computer Network Privacy and Digital Signatures

Encryption, Decryption, and Authentication Techniques

Exploring the fundamental concepts of network privacy through encryption/decryption techniques and the implementation of digital signatures for authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation in computer networks.

Introduction to Computer Network Privacy

The concept of how to achieve privacy has not been changed for thousands of years: the message cannot be encrypted. The message must be rendered as opaque to all the unauthorized parties. A good encryption/decryption technique is used to achieve privacy to some extent.

This technique ensures that the eavesdropper cannot understand the contents of the message.

Encryption and decryption make up the heart of how we protect data these days. The whole idea of encryption involves taking plain text that anyone can read and scrambling it into something called ciphertext that looks like nonsense. Only people who have the right key for decryption get to turn it back into the original stuff.

Encryption and Decryption

Encryption

Encryption means that the sender converts the original information into another form and sends the unintelligible message over the network.

Decryption

Decryption reverses the Encryption process in order to transform the message back to the original form.

The data which is to be encrypted at the sender site is known as plaintext, and the encrypted data is known as ciphertext. The data is decrypted at the receiver site.

There are two types of Encryption/Decryption techniques:

  • Privacy with secret key Encryption/Decryption
  • Privacy with public key Encryption/Decryption

Secret Key Encryption/Decryption Technique

In Secret Key Encryption/Decryption technique, the same key is used by both the parties, i.e., the sender and receiver.

  • The sender uses the secret key and encryption algorithm to encrypt the data; the receiver uses this key and decryption algorithm to decrypt the data.
  • In Secret Key Encryption/Decryption technique, the algorithm used for encryption is the inverse of the algorithm used for decryption.
  • The secret key encryption algorithm is also known as symmetric encryption algorithm because the same secret key is used in bidirectional communication.
  • The secret key requires that we should know which computers are talking to each other so that we can install the key on each computer.

Symmetric and Asymmetric Encryption

Encryption splits into two big kinds. Symmetric and asymmetric, which are the two pillars of data protection and both help lock down digital talks a lot.

🔑

Symmetric Encryption

Symmetric encryption sticks with one key for both the scrambling and unscrambling. It runs quickly and smoothly. People use it in standards like AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) and DES (Data Encryption Standard). Still, the tough part comes with handing out that key.

🔐

Asymmetric Encryption

Asymmetric encryption goes further with two keys. One public key handles the encryption. The private one does the decryption. That setup boosts safety on networks, particularly for sending touchy info over lines that are not secure. Algorithms like RSA and ECC tie those keys together with tough math.

Each type brings its own good points. They often team up in practice. Symmetric handles the fast parts. Asymmetric takes care of swapping keys safely. Together, they mix speed, trust, and solid protection for data.

Encryption Process Explained

The way encryption works follows a step-by-step plan to keep info safe and talks secure:

1

Key Selection

First off, you pick or make a key. That key fits the algorithm you choose. It sets up how everything gets changed and then changed back later.

2

Plaintext Preparation

Then the plain text gets ready. You format that original data for the scramble.

3

Encryption

In the main step, the algorithm grabs the key and turns plain text into ciphertext. That hides the real info from anyone not allowed to see it.

4

Transmission/Storage

Last, you send or store the ciphertext in a safe spot. It could go over a network or sit in a database with locks. Without the right key, it stays confusing.

This whole setup keeps sensitive things hidden and unchanged from start to finish. It cuts down the chances of someone grabbing it, breaking in, or peeking without permission.

Decryption Mechanism

Decryption undoes what encryption did. It changes the ciphertext back to plain text, which makes sense. When the scrambled data shows up, the process starts.

1

Receive Ciphertext

The system receives the encrypted data that needs to be decrypted.

2

Key Application

The system uses the right key and the matching algorithm to read the code.

3

Decryption

The ciphertext is transformed back into readable plaintext.

Only cleared users can get to it and make sense of it. Even if it got snagged in transit, that does not help. The whole thing holds up privacy, realness, and steadiness in safe channels.

Symmetric setups use the same key for both sides. That keeps things speedy and simple. Asymmetric ones need the pair. Public for locking, private for unlocking. It adds more safety layers.

Data Encryption Standard (DES)

The Data Encryption Standard (DES) was designed by IBM and adopted by the U.S. government as the standard encryption method for nonmilitary and nonclassified use.

The Data Encryption Standard is a standard used for encryption, and it is a form of Secret Key Cryptography.

DES uses a 56-bit key to encrypt blocks of data. While it was once a strong standard, it is now considered vulnerable to brute-force attacks due to its relatively short key length. It has been largely replaced by more secure algorithms like AES (Advanced Encryption Standard).

There are two keys in public key encryption: a private key and a public key. The private key is given to the receiver while the public key is provided to the public.

Advantages of Symmetric Encryption

Symmetric encryption runs at high speed. It uses the same key for both encrypting and decrypting, so the whole process ends up much faster than what you get with asymmetric encryption.

⚡

High Speed

Symmetric encryption algorithms are computationally less intensive than asymmetric ones, making them much faster for both encryption and decryption operations.

💾

Resource Efficiency

Symmetric methods need less power for computing and smaller amounts of memory. That makes them perfect for handling big batches of data or working on systems that do not have many resources.

📊

Suitable for Large Data

Because of its efficiency, symmetric encryption is ideal for encrypting large amounts of data, such as files, databases, or streaming media.

Still, symmetric key encryption runs into some real problems despite these strengths. Securely sharing and managing keys becomes tricky, particularly in large networks or ones spread across different places. Asymmetric encryption steps in to handle those kinds of issues.

Disadvantages of Symmetric Encryption

Symmetric encryption runs fast and handles things efficiently. But it has some clear limitations that impact data security and how well it scales up.

Key Distribution Challenge

One major issue comes with distributing the secret key securely between the sender and receiver. If someone intercepts that key while it's being sent, the whole encryption setup gets compromised right away.

Scalability Issues

Scaling this up brings more problems, too. Keeping track of unique keys for a bunch of users turns complicated fast. In a large group, the keys multiply quickly since each pair of people needs their own separate key.

Lack of Authentication

Symmetric encryption also misses out on built-in authentication. It does not check who actually sent the message or if anyone changed the data along the way.

Complex Key Management

Key management adds another layer of hassle over time. Keys need to be rotated or swapped out regularly to stay secure. Handling those updates across different systems and users takes a lot of time and often leads to mistakes.

What is Digital Signature?

The Digital Signature is a technique which is used to validate the authenticity and integrity of the message. We know that there are four aspects of security: privacy, authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation.

We have already discussed the first aspect of security and other three aspects can be achieved by using a digital signature.

The basic idea behind the Digital Signature is to sign a document. When we send a document electronically, we can also sign it. We can sign a document in two ways: to sign a whole document and to sign a digest.

Signing the Whole Document

In Digital Signature, a public key encryption technique is used to sign a document. However, the roles of a public key and private key are different here.

  • The sender uses a private key to encrypt the message while the receiver uses the public key of the sender to decrypt the message.
  • In Digital Signature, the private key is used for encryption while the public key is used for decryption.
  • Digital Signature cannot be achieved by using secret key encryption.

Aspects Achieved by Digital Signatures

Digital Signature is used to achieve the following three aspects:

✅

Integrity

The Digital Signature preserves the integrity of a message because, if any malicious attack intercepts a message and partially or totally changes it, then the decrypted message would be impossible.

👤

Authentication

We can use the following reasoning to show how the message is authenticated. If an intruder (user X) sends a message pretending that it is coming from someone else (user A), user X uses her own private key to encrypt the message. The message is decrypted by using the public key of user A. Therefore this makes the message unreadable.

📝

Non-Repudiation

Digital Signature also provides non-repudiation. If the sender denies sending the message, then her private key corresponding to her public key is tested on the plaintext. If the decrypted message is the same as the original message, then we know that the sender has sent the message.

Note: Digital Signature does not provide privacy. If there is a need for privacy, then another layer of encryption/decryption is applied.

Signing the Digest

Public key encryption is efficient if the message is short. If the message is long, a public key encryption is inefficient to use. The solution to this problem is to let the sender sign a digest of the document instead of the whole document.

The sender creates a miniature version (digest) of the document and then signs it, the receiver checks the signature of the miniature version.

The hash function is used to create a digest of the message. The hash function creates a fixed-size digest from the variable-length message.

The two most common hash functions used: MD5 (Message Digest 5) and SHA-1 (Secure Hash Algorithm 1). The first one produces 120-bit digest while the second one produces 160-bit digest.

A hash function must have two properties to ensure the success:

  • First, the digest must be one way, i.e., the digest can only be created from the message but not vice versa.
  • Second, hashing is a one-to-one function, i.e., two messages should not create the same digest.

Process of Signing the Digest

Following are the steps taken to ensure security:

1

Create Digest

The miniature version (digest) of the message is created by using a hash function.

2

Encrypt Digest

The digest is encrypted by using the sender's private key.

3

Attach and Send

After the digest is encrypted, then the encrypted digest is attached to the original message and sent to the receiver.

4

Verify at Receiver

The receiver receives the original message and encrypted digest and separates the two. The receiver implements the hash function on the original message to create the second digest, and it also decrypts the received digest by using the public key of the sender.

If both the digests are same, then all the aspects of security are preserved.

Working of Digital Signature

Digital signatures utilize a simultaneous combination of cryptographic techniques, hash functions, and key pairs to ensure the authenticity of and to confirm that electronic messages and documents have not been tampered with. We can split the entire process into three main stages, which are Key Generation, Signing, and Verification.

1

Key Generation

In order to create a digital signature, a pair of cryptographic keys to be generated first, which include a public, and a private key. The private key gets used to sign documents, and the owner gets to keep this one secret and to themselves. The public key gets used to authenticate the signature, and this one gets shared with everyone.

2

Signing Process

Whenever a document is to be signed by the sender, the document or message gets sent through a hash function, which gets the message digest, and generates a fixed-length representation. The sender then encrypts this digest using their private key. The result from this type of encryption will be a digital signature. The signed message and the digital signature are sent together and sent to the user.

3

Verification Process

The receiver of the signed message performs the following steps once they have the message: The receiver will apply the same hash function to the message they have received to create a digest. The receiver will use the public key from the sender to decrypt the digital signature to extract the digest they sent. The receiver will check both digests. If they are the same, meaning the message is not altered, and the sender is verified.

Mathematical Representation

To illustrate this process in a more formal way, let the message be M. This message will have the following it a hash function H, such that:

H(M) = h

h will be the digest of the message.

When a person uses the private key PRa and encrypts the message digest:

S = E(PRa, h)

This S is the digital signature.

After receiving M and S, the recipient uses a hash function on the message:

H(M) = h₁

Then, they use the sender's public key PUa to decrypt the signature:

D(PUa, S) = h₂

If h₁ = h₂, the signature is valid. Otherwise, the signature is invalid. The mathematics involved shows the relation of the steps taken to the number of digital signatures.

Digital Signature Algorithms

There are several different algorithms, and each uses varying levels of security, performance, and the algorithms being used are more or less accepted.

1. RSA (Rivest-Shamir-Adleman)

RSA is one of the oldest and most well-used asymmetric algorithms used in cryptography, works with both encryption and digital signatures.

  • Key size: A key size is usually 2048 or 4096 bits.
  • Operation: The signer uses private key exponentiation to sign the hash. The verifier uses public key exponentiation to check validity.
  • Advantages: High compatibility, it is simple, and widely deployed.
  • Disadvantages: The disadvantage of this approach is that it is slower. It is signed with large amounts of data.

2. DSA (Digital Signature Algorithm)

Digital Signature algorithm (DSA) is an algorithm developed by the US's National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) used for digital signatures.

  • Key size: 1024-3072 bits.
  • Operation: It employs concepts of modular arithmetic and discrete logarithm.
  • Advantages: While this algorithm is efficient for signing operations, verification is slow, and therefore, it relies on proper randomness.
  • Disadvantages: A weak random function may lead to the leaking of the signing private key.

More Digital Signature Algorithms

3. ECDSA (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm)

ECDSA is a variant of the DSA that employs elliptic curve (ECC) cryptography, of which the key size is:

  • Key size: 256 to 521 bits (a smaller but stronger key size than RSA).
  • Advantages: The advantages of this algorithm are that it is faster, more secure, and thus more suitable for modern mobile and IoT applications.
  • Disadvantages: ECDSA is more complex to implement than DSA, and this has historically led to patent issues that slow down its adoption.

4. EdDSA (Edwards-curve Digital Signature Algorithm)

The EdDSA is a more recent design based on twisted Edwards curves, e.g., Ed25519.

  • Advantages: EdDSA has a very fast signing and verification time, as well as being resistant to side-channel attacks. It also has a safer default configuration.
  • Disadvantages: Not as universally supported as RSA

Given Systems today's system designs favor the ECDSA and EdDSA algorithms due to better performance and smaller key sizes.

Digital Certificates and Certificate Authorities (CA)

A digital signature is dependent on public key infrastructure (PKI) for key management and authentication, and digital certificates and Certificate Authorities (CAs) are important at this point.

Digital Certificate

A digital certificate is an electronic document that is issued by a trusted authority certifying the association of an individual, an organization, or a device to a public key.

It typically contains:

  • The owner's name and public key
  • Certificate serial number
  • Expiration date
  • Name of the issuing CA
  • The CA's digital signature

A digital certificate guarantees that the public key is indeed that of the individual or organization to whom it is attributed.

Certificate Authority (CA)

A CA is a trusted third party that is responsible for the dissemination and supervision of digital certificates. Some of the popular CAs are DigiCert, Comodo, and GlobalSign.

CAs issue certificates after verifying the identity of the concerned parties, and maintain Certificate Revocation Lists (CRLs) to manage revoked or expired certificates.

Dependence on PKI

Digital signatures put all their trust in Certificate Authorities. A trust framework can be completely collapsed when a CA is breached or acts in a malicious manner.

Revocation and expiration of certificates is always a headache, and becomes more complicated the larger the organization is.

Key Management Issues

All users need to protect their private keys. If a user loses their private key, they can no longer sign documents.

Compatibility Issues

Not all platforms and applications support every standard of digital signatures, which makes interoperability difficult.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. What is a digital signature, and how does it differ from an electronic signature?
Digital signatures are a type of legally accepted cryptographic verification of documents. Digital signatures are created, verified, and secured with a unique cryptography function (e.g., RSA, ECDSA, or EdDSA) and hash functions (e.g., SHA-256). An electronic signature, by contrast, can refer to electronic forms of signatures, such as printed names or scanned signatures, as well as other cryptographically secured options, which do not necessarily give any cryptographic protection.
2. How does a digital signature verify the identity of the signer?
Digital signatures are a verification of the identity of the signer by means of asymmetric encryption, which is a type of cryptography. Each signer is given a unique pair of private and public cryptography keys, which are used to digitally sign and validate documents. The private key is used to sign the document, and the public key is used to validate the signature.
3. Are digital signatures legally valid?
Digital signatures are accepted all over the world as valid signatures, with no alterations to the validity of documents. Digital signatures are legally accepted documents in India (IT Act), the European Union (eIDAS), and in the United States, all legal documents require e e-signature (ESIGN Act). Digital signatures are as legitimate as a handwritten signature if the digital signatures are verified with a digital certificate from a recognized Certificate Authority (CA).
4. What are the main limitations of digital signatures?
Some limitations are dependent on PKI infrastructure, issues with certifying revocation and managing keys, dissimilarities between formats and compatibility, and the cost of implementation for small organizations. Vulnerabilities can be exposed if adequate structures and mechanisms secured keys are not used.
5. Where are digital signatures commonly used?
Digital signatures are integrated with (S/MIME) for email security, software distribution, e-commerce, governmental services, transactions on the blockchain, healthcare records, and legal or monetary documents. They facilitate workflow, lessen the amount of paper used, and trust digital transactions.
